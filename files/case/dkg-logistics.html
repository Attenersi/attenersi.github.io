We started chasing RL papers. We finished writing a heuristic.

A case study of De Keuken Groep's truck loading optimisation. Four months, five students, three rejected approaches, one production deployment — and a data quality journey that mattered more than any of them.

ClientDe Keuken Groep · Kitchen manufacturer · NL
DurationSept 2025 — Jan 2026 · one semester
Team5 students, BUAS Data Science & AI minor

§ 01 The brief

Forty trucks a day. Zero guidance.

De Keuken Groep is a Dutch kitchen manufacturer. They load roughly forty trucks a day with finished cabinets — heavy, fragile, arranged in delivery sequence so the last customer's order can't end up behind the first. The loading process was manual. No systematic plan, no audit trail, no record of whether the warehouse honoured fragile-handling or weight-stacking rules. Their ask: an optimisation tool that automates loading proposals without losing safety.

The constraints stacked fast. LIFO delivery sequence is non-negotiable. Items above 23 kg can't be stacked on lighter ones (Dutch ARBO law). Fragile items can't bear weight. Maximum 2.0 m stack height for safe manual unloading. Six different truck types, six different geometries. And a dataset where the first version we received was 52/100 on quality — missing 91 % of Length values.

This is where the real project started. Not with code. With data.

§ 02 The data void

The first dataset had more holes than data.

For the first six weeks we had a problem nobody wanted to admit: we couldn't build anything. The proposal we wrote in October promised a sophisticated 3D bin packer integrated with SAP. The dataset we received in early October promised something else — a 52/100 quality score, 90.92 % of records missing Length, 71.28 % missing Width, 65.95 % missing Height. Twenty-five percent of records had three usable dimensions. The other seventy-five percent were holes.

I wrote the first Data Quality Report on 7 October. The team reaction was blunt: "this dataset is not a best one." The second dataset arrived a month later. Better — 88.9 % complete — but it contained 121,576 records with Length exactly equal to zero. A cabinet cannot have zero length. We had traded one problem for another.

By the time the third dataset arrived, we had built what we called a fallback strategy. Five dimensional columns instead of the standard three (Length, Width, Height, Depth, Thickness) meant that when primary dimensions were missing, the system could swap from secondary columns. We used Depth to replace missing Length 227,427 times. We used Thickness to replace missing Width 50,788 times. Where even fallback failed, a Random Forest model imputed values from correlated features.

The third DQR closed the loop. Production-ready quality score of 95.8/100. 99.5 % of records with three usable dimensions. Forty-seven days, three iterations, one cleaned dataset.

Data Quality Score — three iterations, 47 days 52.0 → 95.8

This was the only phase where we knew what we were doing.

§ 03 The wrong problem

We spent weeks training models to answer a question DKG had already answered.

In early November we ran into our second problem: we'd misunderstood the question. We had spent weeks training Recurrent Neural Networks to predict the loading sequence. RNNs, LSTMs, the works. The thinking was that an LSTM trained on enough historical orders would learn the implicit patterns the warehouse veterans used.

The results were disheartening. LSTM mean absolute error of 5.52 against a random baseline of 5.84. The model had learned almost nothing.

The pivot came on 4 November. The realisation was that DKG already knew the loading sequence — it was dictated by the delivery route. Customer C gets delivered last, so Customer C's items load first. The sequence wasn't the unknown. The fit was.

"DKG knows the loading sequence. They just don't know how to fit them correctly inside the truck."

We abandoned the deep learning models. The real problem was 3D bin packing.

§ 04 Three failures

The smartest approach kept losing to the simplest one.

What followed was a month of trying things that did not work.

CP-SAT. Mathematically pure. Operationally useless.

Google OR-Tools constraint programming solver. The mathematically pure approach: encode every constraint, let the solver search. For a fifty-item truck this produced thousands of boolean variables and tens of thousands of constraint clauses. With eight workers and ten-minute timeouts, the solver returned solutions that placed items mid-air with no support below. Flying cabinets. Items in physically impossible positions. We documented this as "a big failure" and moved on.

Maskable PPO. The density king without guarantees.

Reinforcement learning, our most ambitious bet. The truck was modelled as a 3D grid, an agent learned through reward signals to pack densely. By iteration three the model was impressive — 45.7 % volume utilisation, 886 of 941 items placed, multi-layer stacks emerging without explicit programming. But two problems killed it: training took hours per iteration, and reinforcement learning is fundamentally probabilistic. We couldn't guarantee fragile-handling compliance. Couldn't prove LIFO would hold for every order. The agent had learned safety as a tendency, not a rule.

DBLF. Deterministic. Compliant. Shipped.

The Deepest-Bottom-Left-Fill heuristic, built last. Items sorted by sequence (highest number first, since those go deepest in the truck), then by weight class, then by volume. Each item placed greedily in the deepest, lowest, leftmost feasible position. Hard constraints checked as filters, not penalties — if a placement violated stacking rules it was rejected outright. Deterministic. Identical inputs always produced identical outputs. Execution time: under a minute for a thousand-item order. Constraint compliance: guaranteed by construction.

DBLF won not because it was the smartest approach. It won because in a domain governed by hard physical rules — Dutch ARBO law, fragility, LIFO — guarantees matter more than density. A 45-percent-packed truck that occasionally crushes glass is worse than a 33-percent-packed truck that never does.

"RL was the density king. It just couldn't promise it wouldn't break the law."
§ 05 The tablet moment

The warehouse doesn't have time for our 3D dashboard.

In mid-November we showed Jeroen, our contact at DKG, the interactive 3D dashboard we had been building. A user could rotate the truck, zoom into a corner, click on a cabinet to see its metadata. We were proud of it. Jeroen's response was three words too short to soften.

"Visualisation might not work — it's too complicated."

He explained. The expedition team — the actual humans loading the trucks — have heavy cabinets in their arms and dirty hands. They don't swipe tablets. They don't rotate 3D models to find a hidden box. They need the plan row by row, on paper, while they're carrying.

We pivoted within a week. The new deliverable was a landscape-oriented PDF checklist with six items per page, colour swatches that matched a 3D image at the side, and tags for FRAGILE (red) and HEAVY (>30 kg). Each cabinet got a printable sticker — Article ID, sequence number ("SEQ 13"), weight, and THIS SIDE UP arrows. The interactive 3D viewer stayed, but it was now for office planning. The warehouse got paper.

That conversation was the moment the project stopped being about algorithms and started being about a product.

§ 06 Validation

What the final numbers looked like — and what they didn't measure.

The final system was validated against 2,230 historical transport orders containing 1,018,368 items.

Avg. packing rate 98.61% vs. 100% manual baseline
LIFO compliance 99%+ manual baseline: untracked
Perfect-match orders 41.4% 923 of 2,230
Within 5% gap 95.6% 2,132 of 2,230
Constraint violations 0 fragile, weight, height
Runtime ~2 min per 500–1000 item order

The 1.39 % packing gap deserves its own line. It is not a failure of the algorithm. It is the cost of guaranteed compliance. Manual loaders can tilt items at an angle, compress soft packaging, or bend LIFO rules to squeeze the last cabinet in. The algorithm cannot. We chose to ship the version that refuses to crush glass.

§ 07 My role

I wasn't the algorithm person. I was the product person.

This was a five-person team. The DBLF heuristic was Ashraf's. The RL model was Mohamed's. The core data preprocessing modules were Mark's. The other contributions, in order of effort, were mine.

What I owned
  • Three iterations of Data Quality Reports — moved the dataset from 25.5 % to 99.5 % usable records
  • The Business Requirements Document and interactive Traceability Matrix — what we promised, traced to what we shipped
  • The first Streamlit prototype — connected the early RL model to a working 3D viewer; later rebuilt with a Bottom-Left heuristic
  • The ethical & legal layer — Terms of Use, the "Reality Gap" checklist, the kill-switch liability waiver, the "Decision Support System" framing that defined what the software was and wasn't
  • Power BI dashboards — Models Comparison & Managers' view
  • Scrum Master in the final sprint when everything had to converge
  • The ethical & future-recommendations portion of the final client presentation

What I took from this.

Sophistication is not a virtue. In a domain governed by hard constraints, the "smartest" model is the one that can prove it won't break a rule, not the one that scores highest on a benchmark. The deterministic answer was the right answer. We just had to fail three times to see it.

The other lesson is older and I should have known it from six years on a warehouse floor: the people doing the work tell you when your interface is wrong, and they tell you in one sentence. Jeroen needed three words. We listened.
